# Engineer Staffing Guide for Vietnam Production Line Migration

Moving a production line from coastal China to Vietnam has stopped being a question of "whether" and become a question of "how to do it without things falling apart." Disassembly, re-layout, re-commissioning — every step needs an automation engineer, and specifically one who understands the equipment you are moving. The step where migrations most often go wrong is rarely the hardware itself. It is arriving in Vietnam and not being able to find someone who can get the line running again. This guide lays out how to staff engineers for a Vietnam production line migration.

## Two things that define local talent in Vietnam

**First, the platform landscape skews Siemens.** Vietnam is a major link in the export supply chain to Europe, and many factories there were originally built to supply European customers — so equipment tends to run Siemens (S7-1500, TIA Portal), with a meaningful presence of Mitsubishi as well. If the equipment you are relocating is already Siemens-based, that is good news: matching local talent is relatively plentiful. The reverse is also true — if your equipment is Rockwell (Allen-Bradley), you will hit a scarcity premium in Vietnam: hiring takes longer and costs more.

**Second, the talent pool is maturing fast, but there is a real gap at the senior end.** As manufacturing keeps relocating into Vietnam, the number of young engineers is growing quickly, and basic L1-level hands are not hard to find. What is relatively scarce is L2 engineers who can independently carry a full line through commissioning, and L3 engineers who can handle hard problems and architecture. Migration commissioning is exactly the phase that needs the most senior hands.

## Rate range: Vietnam's cost advantage

Based on self-reported rates from active engineers on the platform, experienced automation engineers in the Vietnam region run roughly **$30–55/hr**. That is competitive within Southeast Asia, and it is one of the cost dividends of moving a line south. But as with any low rate, it only converts into low total cost when scope is clear, skill is verified, and payment is protected — all three at once.

## Staffing the migration: three phases

A production line migration is not a single event; staffing should follow the phases:

1. **Migration planning and teardown documentation.** Before the move, it is worth having an engineer who knows the equipment produce a "current-state snapshot" — program backups, I/O lists, parameters, wiring records. Done well, this step saves a large amount of rework when reassembling in Vietnam. It can largely be done remotely, with a small amount of on-site time.
2. **Re-layout and reassembly commissioning.** Once the equipment reaches Vietnam, it needs to be reinstalled, rewired, powered up, and re-run. This is the most hands-on-experience-intensive phase, and it needs L2/L3-level people on site, working alongside local engineers.
3. **Line integration and stability acceptance.** A single machine running once does not count as done. The full line needs to run together and hold its takt time for a stretch — a run-off / SAT — before it counts as delivered. Do not declare victory the first time it runs.

A cost-effective combination is to have one senior engineer lead the planning and critical commissioning, while local L1/L2 engineers carry most of the hands-on execution — controlling cost while protecting quality.

## Why verified skill matters especially in a migration

Migration commissioning is high-pressure, time-boxed work where mistakes are expensive. Someone whose résumé simply says "Siemens TIA Portal" gives you no way to confirm they can actually handle the parts that hide bugs — optimized data blocks, safety configuration, communication handshakes. Cross-border hiring makes background checks harder and résumés easier to inflate, which is exactly why a verification layer is not optional.

## Design cross-language, cross-timezone collaboration up front

A team from the Chinese side, local Vietnamese engineers, and possibly the original equipment vendor — multi-party collaboration with language gaps and a modest timezone offset is the norm, not the exception. Writing requirements down and requiring a short daily written progress update is far more reliable than a half-understood phone call. The platform's built-in nine-language real-time-translation project workspace lets Chinese and Vietnamese (plus English) be mutually legible in the same interface, cutting down on "misunderstood scope" — the quiet killer of migration projects.

## Escrow payment: let the process carry cross-border trust

A migration runs long and moves real money, which makes cross-border payment trust an even bigger issue. Milestone escrow keeps the logic simple: fund each phase's payment into escrow when it starts, and release it once you have approved the deliverable. You never have to wire a stranger a large sum up front, and the engineer never has to worry about finishing the work and not getting paid. Set the three phases above — plan, reassemble, integrate — as three milestones, and your payment cadence naturally lines up with your delivery cadence.

## Summary

Staffing a Vietnam production line migration comes down to three things: **hire around the equipment platform (Siemens dominates in Vietnam), pair senior hands with local talent by certification level, and hand cross-border risk to milestone escrow plus nine-language collaboration rather than trust alone.** Break the migration into three milestones — plan, reassemble, integrate — each with acceptance criteria, and you get both your project plan and your payment plan in one structure.
